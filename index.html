Think→Make→See→Learn→Refine→Ship

That's pretty much how I've always worked. Start with the idea. Make enough of it to see what's there. Put it in front of people. Learn something. Make it better.

The tools keep changing. The process really hasn't.

Role / approach

I like being close to the work.

I've spent my career moving between strategy and execution — setting direction, building teams, developing ideas and then getting close enough to the work to help make those ideas real.

Sometimes that means defining the strategy. Sometimes it's finding the concept. Sometimes it's directing the experience, film, photography or story. Sometimes it's making the prototype myself.

And sometimes it's creating the space for someone on my team to see the answer I couldn't.

The role changes. The responsibility doesn't:

Make the work better.

Leadership

Making is also how I lead.

The best creative leadership isn't about having all the ideas. It's about creating an environment where great ideas can come from anywhere — and helping talented people develop the confidence, judgment and craft to make them real.

I've had the privilege of helping people grow into bigger roles, find their creative voice, take risks, lead teams and make work they didn't know they were capable of making.

Sometimes leadership means pushing. Sometimes protecting an idea. Sometimes removing an obstacle. Sometimes getting out of the way.

And sometimes it's simply saying: You can do this.

Some of the work I'm proudest of has someone else's name on it.

Things I've learned about making things with people.

    Impact / people

    Great work leaves something behind.

    Products are one measure of impact. People are another.

    Over the years I've led creative and product design teams through change, ambiguity, new technologies and some very difficult problems.

    What matters to me isn't simply what those teams shipped. It's whether people did work they were proud of. Whether they grew. Whether they felt trusted. Whether they discovered something they didn't know they were capable of. Whether they wanted to do it again.

    AI / rapid prototyping

    My hands finally move as fast as my head.

    I've always had more ideas than I could make. Software traditionally put distance between imagining something and experiencing it. An idea needed resources, explanation, prioritization, engineering and time before you could really understand whether it worked.

    AI is collapsing that distance.

    Today I use AI as part of my design process to move from an idea to a working prototype incredibly quickly — something I can see, touch, test, break and make better.

    Instead of asking “What if?” we can ask “What do you think of this?”

    I design. I prototype. I experiment. I test. I learn. I break things. I start again.

    AI hasn't made design less important. It's made taste more important.

    And it's made me more excited about making than I've been in years.

    Copilot / AI at scale

    Making at a different scale.

    At Microsoft, I'm currently leading creative and product design work in the context of Copilot — partnering across disciplines to explore, experiment, test and launch AI experiences designed to make people more capable and productive.

    We're simultaneously designing products and discovering what designing AI products even means.

    That requires strategy and systems thinking. It requires experimentation. It requires listening closely to customers. It requires teams willing to make something, learn that they're wrong, and make it again.

    And increasingly, it requires leaders who can move comfortably between all of those things.

    That's where I like to work.

    02 / A little history

    I've been doing this for a while.

    Long enough to watch the definition of “designer” change more than once. I started in New York agencies, where I learned about ideas, storytelling, art direction, craft and the sometimes uncomfortable art of convincing someone else to believe in something that doesn't exist yet. Then I came to Microsoft and learned what happens when an idea has to work at enormous scale.

    New York

    I learned to sell the idea.

    Before product design, there was advertising. The work taught me to start with the idea rather than the artifact.

    Why should anyone care? What's the story? What's the thing people will remember?

    It also taught me that craft matters — because even a great idea can disappear inside mediocre execution.

    Microsoft

    Then I learned to scale the idea.

    Products became ecosystems. Audiences became millions of people. Creative decisions had technological, organizational and business consequences.

    Over more than two decades, my role evolved from making the work myself to setting creative direction, shaping product experiences, building and leading teams, and helping organizations turn ambiguity into something people could actually use.

    But I never really stopped being a maker.

    Tectonic

    I left.

    At one point, I stepped outside Microsoft and joined Tectonic. After years inside one of the world's largest technology companies, changing environments gave me the chance to question what I'd learned — and what I'd simply gotten used to.

    Return to Microsoft

    Then I came back.

    Not to repeat the first chapter. To bring something different to the next one.

    The industry was changing. So was Microsoft. And so was I.

    The through line

    The tools changed. The thing I care about didn't.

    I've worked through enough technological shifts to know that the medium will keep changing. What survives is the ability to recognize an interesting idea, understand why someone should care about it, bring talented people around it and make it real enough to learn from.

    03 / The lab

    I can't really stop making things.

    Apps. Interfaces. AI experiments. Music. Objects. Brands. Prototypes. Weird ideas.

    Some become products. Some teach me something. Some are just fun. And that's kind of the point.

    The Lab is where I make without waiting for permission. An idea can become a prototype tonight. A prototype can become an app next week. Or it can fail spectacularly and teach me something useful.

    Curiosity doesn't need a roadmap.

    Why the lab matters

    Making keeps me honest.

    Staying hands-on changes the way I lead. I understand what today's tools make possible because I'm using them. I understand where the friction is because I'm experiencing it.

    I can put something tangible in front of a team instead of describing it in a deck. And I can recognize exceptional craft because I'm still practicing the craft myself.

    I don't think leadership and making are opposites. I think one makes me better at the other.

    What's next

    I don't think the interesting part has happened yet.

    I've spent my career watching the tools change. Advertising became interactive. The web became software. Software became mobile. Mobile became ambient. And now AI is changing what it means to make almost anything.

    I'm interested in what happens next — especially the messy part before everyone agrees what the answer is. The problems without an obvious solution. The ideas that are easier to show than explain. The teams willing to experiment until something clicks.

    Give me smart people, an important problem, new tools and enough room to make something. We'll figure it out.
